# Pixel Ops: a review

**Score: 3.5 out of 5.**

A browser RTS that crosses the command layer of Squad with the infantry model of
Close Combat, built for one player against a CPU commander. The simulation is
deeper than anything in its weight class. The solo-only scope and prototype
presentation keep it from a higher score.

## How a match works

You begin in a force builder with a points budget. Spend it on squads such as
rifle, support, anti-tank, medic, marksman and engineer, on vehicles from trucks
and Humvees up to APCs, Abrams tanks, Chinooks and Little Bird gunships, and on
one doctrine that shapes how you fight. The CPU reads your loadout and
counter-picks.

There is no resource gathering and no tech tree. Each side starts with a pool of
tickets. Every respawn, lost vehicle and lost objective drains it, and zero means
you lose on the spot. Your economy is men and machines you cannot afford to
waste.

The default mode is Advance and Secure. Objectives sit on a lattice and are
captured in order. Holding the majority bleeds the enemy. Taking them all wins
outright. Matches run about 25 minutes.

Controls are classic RTS. Left-drag selects, right-click moves, attack-move and
hold fire do what you expect, and control groups work. On top of that sits your
own CO, an AI officer who runs the squads you have not taken in hand. You give it
orders through a Command Wheel or in plain words, typed or spoken, and it answers
over the radio.

## What sets it apart

- **The firefight.** Men fire single aimed shots and short bursts, accuracy falls
  with range, and line of sight is required. A man behind a wall is hit half as
  often. A hit is a casualty, dead or wounded, and the wounded can be patched up
  by a squadmate or a medic or bleed out where they fell. Squads have morale and
  go shaken, then pinned, then broken. Men carry magazines and run dry. The result
  is fire and movement rather than a blob rush.
- **Logistics that matter.** Supply trucks feed engineers who build forward
  bases, sandbag positions, machine gun, anti-tank and mortar emplacements, and
  tank traps that close a bridge. Rally points let a squad respawn forward.
  Trucks, APCs and helicopters carry troops, and on Peninsula the attacker comes
  ashore from an assault ship by landing craft.
- **Fog of war with teeth.** Your side sees only what its units see, hills and
  buildings block sight, and the CPU plays by the same rules.
- **An opponent that thinks.** The default commander is a utility AI that reads
  your style and adjusts its posture. You can swap in a large language model
  commander, Llama or Claude, which writes plans and talks on the radio. Three
  difficulty levels change its aim, respawns and aggression.
- **Six maps, six modes.** Palm Valley, Desert Crossing, Narva, Peninsula, The
  Hollow and Hilltop Farm range from a 3 km river city to a 500 m infantry-only
  wood. Modes are Advance and Secure, Invasion, Destruction, Territory Control,
  Skirmish and a wave-defence Hold mode.
- **Runs anywhere.** No install, no build step, and it plays on an iPad with a
  full touch layer.

## Where it falls short

- **Solo only.** No multiplayer, no campaign, no ladder. Replay value is the maps
  and the CPU's variety.
- **The look.** Blocky voxel models on flat-shaded ground. The pixel-art
  rendering is a clever trick, but the world reads as a prototype. One faction,
  with the enemy a recolour.
- **The learning curve.** The field manual is mandatory, the HUD is dense and
  there are dozens of keys. Your first match will be spent reading.
- **The pace.** A cautious CPU, slow respawns and 25-minute matches. Players
  raised on fast macro RTS will find it ponderous.
- **Rough edges.** Peninsula is still a blockout, Desert Crossing favours one
  side, and integrated GPUs judder at play zoom with shadows on.

## Who it is for

Players who liked Close Combat, Men of War or Squad's command role, and who want
a thoughtful single-player fight in a browser tab. Players who want polish,
multiplayer or a StarCraft tempo should wait.
